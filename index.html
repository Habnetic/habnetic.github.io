Inference · Constrained selection · Decision reliability

How reliable are decisions made under uncertainty?

Models inform decisions, but evidence is incomplete, assumptions matter and resources are limited. Habnetic investigates how uncertainty affects the choices we make and how confidently those choices can be defended.

The first experimental case studies Bayesian top-k selection in urban flood-risk prioritisation across Rotterdam, Hamburg and Donostia–San Sebastián. It measures stability under fitted models; whether those choices are correct in practice remains a separate question for validation.

Why decision stability?

Uncertainty matters when it can change a decision

Uncertain estimates

In the flood-risk experiment, estimates vary across posterior draws. Those differences do not always change which assets are selected.

→

Decision rule

A fixed-capacity rule converts estimates into selections: in each posterior draw, assets are ranked and the top k are selected.

→

Stable or uncertain selection

An asset's posterior top-k membership probability distinguishes consistent selections from an uncertain decision boundary.

Stability is not the same as correctness. A decision can remain unchanged across posterior draws and still be poorly informed if the data, model assumptions or decision rule are unsuitable. Decision reliability also depends on evidence quality and the consequences of being wrong.

Experimental findings

What the experiments show

◎

Most assets lie outside the uncertain membership boundary.

In Rotterdam, with approximately 1% prioritisation capacity, only 30 of 221,324 buildings (0.0136%) have posterior top-k membership probabilities between 0.2 and 0.8.

⌁

Instability concentrates near the selection boundary.

Intermediate membership probabilities are concentrated near the top-k cut-off rather than across the entire asset ranking.

⌕

Controlled hazard perturbations cause limited broadening.

In a 50,000-building Rotterdam subsample, the median uncertain-boundary count rises from 10 to 11 at the largest tested perturbation levels.

◈

The structure persists across different urban inputs.

Independent Hamburg and Donostia–San Sebastián fits retain localised decision boundaries under the same specification, although their widths differ.

Research scope. Phase 3 uses simplified exposure and hazard proxies, a Bayesian logistic model and synthetic outcomes. Rotterdam, Hamburg and Donostia–San Sebastián were fitted independently under a fixed specification: this was a cross-city stress test, not a transfer of posterior information between cities. The results investigate selection stability under specified assumptions, not validated flood probabilities, observed building-level losses or operational decision accuracy.

Research roadmap

Strengthening the evidence, then testing what can generalise

The published flood-risk experiments establish a starting point, not a general validation of the methodology. The next phases distinguish work in progress from research questions that have yet to be tested.

Phase 4 · In progress

Evidence quality and cross-domain testing

Part A develops the flood-risk test bed through topography (M1), hazard uncertainty (M2), exposure, vulnerability and consequences (M3), and independent validation (M4). Reproduction and audit of Phase 3 (M0) are complete. Part B is exploring a second decision domain, with building simulation and satellite observation planning as candidates. Cross-domain results are not yet established.

Phase 5 · Proposed

Probabilistic knowledge transfer

Investigate whether evidence from data-rich places can improve inference and decisions where observations are scarce. Possible approaches include hierarchical Bayesian models and partial pooling, with explicit tests of compatibility and negative transfer. Unlike the independent city fits in Phase 3, this would involve transferring probabilistic information between settings.

Research directions are hypotheses and design intentions, not demonstrated performance, validated services or confirmed institutional collaborations.

About Habnetic

Open research on reliable decisions under uncertainty

Habnetic is an independent, open-source research project on probabilistic inference, selection under constraints and the reliability of decisions made with imperfect evidence.

Urban flood-risk prioritisation is the first experimental case, not the limit of the project. Ongoing work aims to improve evidence and validation; future research may test generalisation across domains and probabilistic knowledge transfer between data-rich and data-scarce settings.

  • Bayesian inference, diagnostics and predictive checks
  • Constrained selection and posterior decision stability
  • Independent cross-city tests (published)
  • Knowledge transfer and cross-domain tests (proposed)

About the researcher

Mikel Martínez Mugica

Architect · Independent researcher · Computational and probabilistic modelling

Mikel Martínez Mugica is an architect and independent researcher based in Zürich, Switzerland. His work spans architectural practice, computational design, digital construction methods and probabilistic modelling.

He holds a Master of Advanced Studies (MAS) in History and Theory of Architecture from ETH Zürich and has taught CAD and Building Information Modelling (BIM) at the University of Liechtenstein.

He was part of the winning B22 team for the 2010 AAA Architetticercasi competition at Cascina Merlata, Milan, alongside Stefano Tropea and Sergio Coll. The resulting residential building was subsequently built.

He co-founded non-plan with Katrin Milanzi. Their work contributed to the EWASTE – Burden and Treasure exhibition presented at the 2023 Venice Architecture Biennale in collaboration with the University of Liechtenstein. He has also been involved in the Open Source Construction community.

His more recent professional work focuses on data-driven building valuation and probabilistic modelling. He founded Habnetic to investigate how uncertainty in statistical models propagates into planning and prioritisation decisions.
